# PHOWA: THE GREAT CONSCIOUSNESS HEIST

A very irreverent, philosophically rigorous reading of the Six Dharmas of Nāropa and the Trika Śaivism of Abhinavagupta

*Death, reincarnation, subtle bodies, the apparent escape of consciousness, entering other bodies, buddhafields, the recognition of Śiva, and the absolutely outrageous question of whether anybody actually goes anywhere.*

The first major discovery from the primary sources is that **both traditions really do contain elaborate accounts of consciousness leaving the body.** This isn't merely modern New Age speculation.

In the Tibetan Buddhist tradition, phowa (*'pho ba*\) belongs to the transmitted Six Dharmas, alongside the much more extraordinary practice of entering another body (*grong 'jug*\). The Indian instructions attributed to Tilopa explicitly discuss both. \[[Readings On the Six Yogas of Naropa](https://www.shambhala.com/snowlion_articles/readings-on-the-six-yogas-of-naropa/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

In Trika, Abhinavagupta's *Tantrasāra* describes initiation at the moment of death (Chapter 15), the liberation of the deceased through ritual (Chapter 16), and postmortem rites (Chapter 19). These are genuine, textually attested teachings, not a loose analogy with Tibetan Buddhism. \[[Abhinavagupta: Tantrasara](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/1_sanskr/6_sastra/3_phil/saiva/abhtantu.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Tantrasāra: Chapter 19](https://www.sanskrit-trikashaivism.com/index.php?c=en%2Fscriptures-trika-scriptures-tantrasara-chapter-19%2F951&utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

But the most fascinating problem is philosophical.

**For Buddhism, no permanent self exists to be transferred. For Trika, the deepest Self is Śiva-consciousness that was never confined to the body in the first place.**

So what the bloody hell is travelling?

That question will govern the entire reading.

## I. Congratulations, you're dying. What now?

Right, you magnificent collection of borrowed atoms. Welcome to the most inconvenient appointment on your calendar.

Death.

You've been putting it off your entire life. You've attended retreats, studied Sanskrit, contemplated emptiness, perhaps convinced yourself that consciousness is fundamental to reality.

Beautiful.

The universe has some administrative news.

Your heart is going to stop.

Your lungs are going to stop.

Your elaborate autobiographical identity, with its preferences, anxieties, opinions, ambitions and passionately defended metaphysical theories, is going to lose its ordinary biological support.

And the tantric traditions ask an absolutely extraordinary question\:

*Does the collapse of the body necessarily entail the collapse of every possibility of consciousness?*

Notice that this is not yet the claim that consciousness survives death. It is a question about the relationship between knowing and the physical conditions through which knowing ordinarily manifests.

We mustn't cheat by sneaking our preferred answer into the question.

The Buddhist begins by examining experience as a causally dependent process. The Trika Śaiva begins from the fundamental reality of self-revealing consciousness, *prakāśa*, inseparable from its capacity for reflexive awareness, *vimarśa*\.

Both investigate the apparent individual. But they disagree about the ultimate nature of what is revealed.

And then, because Indian tantric history is gloriously incapable of leaving anything at the level of a sensible philosophical disagreement, somebody asks\:

*Can we actually direct the process of dying?*

Somebody else adds\:

*Could we arrange for consciousness to leave through the top of the head?*

And the next absolute lunatic goes\:

*Could I enter somebody else's body instead?*

Welcome to the death technology department. The syllabus has escalated rather quickly.

These claims form part of historical religious and contemplative systems; their supernatural outcomes haven't been scientifically established. We're examining their philosophical and textual structure, not treating them as verified physical mechanisms or providing instructions for inducing death or consciousness ejection.

## II. Nāropa's six yogas: the whole bloody thing is connected

Tilopa, Nāropa, Marpa, the Kagyu transmissions, and the later Tibetan systems

Research basis: the transmitted *Oral Instruction of the Six Yogas*, translated by Glenn Mullin; the traditional classifications documented by Study Buddhism; and Dr. Alexander Berzin's explanations of completion-stage practice. \[[Readings On the Six Yogas of Naropa](https://www.shambhala.com/snowlion_articles/readings-on-the-six-yogas-of-naropa/?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Six yogas of Naropa](https://studybuddhism.com/en/glossary/six-yogas-of-naropa?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Six Yogas of Naropa: Further Advanced Practices](https://studybuddhism.com/en/advanced-studies/vajrayana/tantra-advanced/introduction-to-the-six-yogas-of-naropa/six-yogas-of-naropa-further-advanced-practices?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

First, a misconception needs to die before we do.

The Six Yogas aren't six unrelated mystical party tricks that Nāropa collected because his meditation retreat was getting boring.

They're an integrated system of *completion-stage* $*niṣpannakrama*, Tibetan *rdzogs rim*$ Vajrayāna practice.

They're concerned with the extraordinary relationship between appearance, mind, subtle energy, embodiment, dreaming and dying.

Different transmissions count the six differently. One common arrangement has inner heat, illusory body, dream, clear light, intermediate state and transference. Another counts transference and entering another body separately, placing dream yoga within the illusory-body training.

That distinction matters because **the yoga of entering another body is not simply an internet legend appended to the tradition**\. It appears within the actual classical lists.

Now follow the architecture.

### Inner heat: the body is not just a meat prison

*Tummo* $*gtum mo*, Sanskrit *caṇḍālī*$.

In the tantric account, ordinary conscious experience depends on patterns of subtle winds, *rlung*, operating within the body's channels.

These winds are not merely the air moving through your nostrils.

They are elements of a traditional psychophysical model in which the dynamism of consciousness and subtle energy are intimately associated.

The point of inner-heat practice is not merely to get sufficiently warm to impress tourists in the Himalayas.

It supplies a foundation for transforming the relation between mind, energy, bliss and cognition.

For certain completion-stage systems, the crucial achievement is the stabilization of increasingly subtle levels of mind while ordinary conceptual activity quiets.

Imagine discovering that your everyday thinking apparatus is merely the noisiest department in a much larger organization.

The chief executive has never personally answered an email.

### Illusory body: reality is not appearing quite as you think

*Gyulü*, the illusory body.

Here the yogin examines the way appearances can be vividly present without possessing the independent, self-established existence ordinarily projected onto them.

A dream mountain is experienced as a mountain. A reflected face appears in a mirror. A rainbow has a determinate appearance without being a solid object waiting for somebody to put it in a shipping container.

But don't mistake the examples for a claim that the waking world is literally nothing or that suffering is imaginary.

The specifically Buddhist philosophical point concerns dependent existence, not indiscriminate denial of appearances.

Why should somebody preparing for death care?

Because if you've spent eighty years identifying yourself with one particular organization of appearances, you're going to encounter a rather significant crisis when that organization dissolves.

Illusory-body yoga loosens the conviction that the ordinary embodied self is the only possible way experience could be structured.

### Dream yoga: training for the disappearance of the familiar world

*Milam*, dream yoga.

Every night, the ordinary waking world vanishes from experience.

Another world appears.

You may acquire a new body, meet strange beings, experience terror, pleasure, confusion, or spend twenty minutes trying to get through an airport despite never having purchased a ticket.

And generally, you believe the whole thing.

Until, occasionally, recognition occurs.

*This is a dream.*

The remarkable event isn't necessarily that the scenery changes.

It's that the relationship to the scenery changes.

The practitioner cultivates recognition within a world whose appearances no longer conform to waking expectations.

This is why dream yoga connects naturally with the *bardo*, the intermediate state described in Tibetan Buddhist traditions.

The analogy is that both concern vivid appearances encountered outside ordinary waking embodiment.

Whether the postmortem bardo exists as described is a separate metaphysical question. Dreaming doesn't prove it.

Nevertheless, the contemplative logic is coherent: if one can recognize deceptive appearances as appearances, one might be less overwhelmed when familiar conditions collapse.

### Clear light: the thing everyone's actually after

*Ösel*, luminosity or clear light.

Now we reach the serious business.

In the Guhyasamāja-derived completion-stage explanation presented by Berzin, the subtle energy-winds and corresponding modes of mental activity are understood to undergo progressive dissolution.

The manifest activity of ordinary perception and thought subsides until the most subtle level of mind becomes apparent.

This is the *clear-light mind*\.

Not a little incandescent soul.

Not a bright white screen inside your skull.

And not automatically enlightenment.

The subtle luminosity itself and the **wisdom realizing its emptiness of inherent existence** must be distinguished.

That last point is essential. A subtle state of awareness may still be accompanied by ignorance. Its subtlety alone does not make it liberating.

The tradition distinguishes the clear light naturally manifest at death from the meditative realization cultivated during life.

Their meeting is sometimes called the recognition of the *mother and child luminosities*\.

The mother is not some cosmic lady hiding behind Saturn.

The metaphor concerns the recognition of what arises naturally at death through the familiarity developed in contemplative practice.

And this leads directly to the yogic understanding of death as a possible opportunity for liberation.

### Bardo: what if the show carries on?

*Bar do* means an intermediate condition.

In the death-and-rebirth context, it refers to the interval between lives as understood in Tibetan Buddhist systems.

The old body's functioning has ended.

The next birth has not yet taken place.

The continuing mental process is understood to encounter appearances influenced by karmic dispositions.

The disturbing implication is that death does not necessarily make you wise.

You might lose your body and retain your ignorance.

Imagine spending decades being a neurotic bastard, dying, and discovering that the neurosis has retained its subscription.

No physical brain required, according to the traditional account. Absolutely appalling customer service.

The point of bardo yoga is to bring recognition and freedom into that unstable process rather than simply undergo it helplessly.

Which brings us to the sixth yoga.

### Phowa: what if you could redirect the exit?

*'Pho ba* - transference.

Now the yogin isn't merely preparing to recognize what arises.

The practitioner is attempting, within the tantric model, to direct the continuity of consciousness toward liberation or an auspicious rebirth.

One traditional destination is the buddhafield of Amitābha, *Sukhāvatī*, Tibetan *Dewachen*\.

And the crucial question becomes\:

**What would it mean to transfer consciousness without transferring an eternal self?**

That's the philosophical grenade at the center of the whole system.

## III. Phowa: the metaphysics of leaving

Imagine the classical tantric model of the dying practitioner.

The ordinary body's ability to support conscious life is failing. The channels, winds and concentrations of awareness are interpreted through an intricate yogic anatomy.

The central channel, usually called *avadhūtī* in the Sanskrit tantric vocabulary, occupies a privileged place.

At the crown of the head lies the symbolic and ritual significance of the *brahmarandhra*, the upper aperture.

The phowa literature describes consciousness in luminous, sometimes syllabic form, and its transference toward a deity or buddhafield.

These descriptions belong to the visualization and subtle-body framework of the practice; the channels should not simply be identified with anatomical nerves or blood vessels.

In the Tilopa-attributed instructions translated by Glenn Mullin, consciousness is directed toward the deity inseparable from the guru and then toward a desired buddhafield. The same source also discusses entering another body. \[[Readings On the Six Yogas of Naropa](https://www.shambhala.com/snowlion_articles/readings-on-the-six-yogas-of-naropa/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Now understand why the crown matters.

In a range of older South Asian sources, including the Upaniṣadic and Śaiva traditions, different bodily exit-points are associated with different postmortem destinies.

The uppermost route comes to signify a particularly favorable departure.

The ritual innovation is extraordinary.

A descriptive theory becomes an intervention.

Rather than merely asking, "Where did the consciousness leave?", the tantric practitioner asks, "Can the conditions be deliberately arranged so that its departure takes the auspicious route?"

**The distinction is between predicting the outcome of death and attempting to transform it.**

Kris Anderson's Berkeley study of Buddhist *utkrānti* rituals documents precisely this broader historical relationship, including important parallels with Śaiva ritual technologies. \[[UC Berkeley](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4zr3j0m9/qt4zr3j0m9.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

But an uncomfortable problem immediately appears.

Suppose phowa is successful, according to the tradition.

Does that mean a small conscious entity flies through space like a spiritual tennis ball?

No. At least, it would be an extremely poor account of the more philosophically sophisticated Buddhist interpretations.

And here our reading gets properly interesting.

## IV. The Buddhist nightmare: who the fuck is transferring?

Right. Philosophy exam.

You have a consciousness.

You perform phowa.

Your consciousness supposedly transfers into Amitābha's buddhafield.

Wonderful.

Now explain what you mean by **your consciousness**\.

The Buddha is not going to let you sneak an immortal soul into the back of Vajrayāna wearing a fake moustache.

The foundational Buddhist doctrine of *anātman* denies a permanent, independently existing self underlying the changing processes of life.

Even *vijñāna*, consciousness, is not a timeless personal substance in early Buddhist analysis.

Indeed, the Buddha explicitly criticizes the notion that numerically identical consciousness simply wanders unchanged from life to life. \[[Mind in Indian Buddhist Philosophy (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mind-indian-buddhism/index.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Buddha](https://iep.utm.edu/buddha/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

So if the same entity does not transmigrate, how could rebirth occur?

Here is the crucial distinction.

**Causal continuity does not require the persistence of an unchanging substance.**

Consider a flame igniting another flame.

We can meaningfully identify a causal relationship. The second flame did not arise from nothing, independently of the first.

Yet we don't need to postulate an immortal little fire-person jumping across the gap.

Or consider your consciousness from childhood to the present.

Your perceptions, memories, intentions, body and personality have changed enormously.

You don't need to establish that every psychological event is numerically identical to some unchanging mental substance in order to explain why your present circumstances depend upon earlier events.

The Buddhist extension of this reasoning beyond death is metaphysical and controversial, but internally intelligible.

One moment of consciousness conditions another.

Karmic dispositions participate in that continuity.

The continuity is conventionally designated as belonging to a person, although no independent, changeless personal essence can be identified within it.

This isn't to say all Buddhist schools explain continuity identically.

Certain Yogācāra systems invoke *ālayavijñāna*, the storehouse consciousness, to explain the preservation of karmic potentials. But they do not intend it as an immortal ātman. Other systems give different accounts, and Tibetan Gelug explanations of the subtlest mind cannot simply be collapsed into the Yogācāra storehouse model. \[[Yogācāra (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy/Fall 2025 Edition)](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2025/entries/yogacara/?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [The Guhyasamaja Presentation of the Process of Death](https://studybuddhism.com/en/advanced-studies/vajrayana/tantra-advanced/the-process-of-death-bardo-and-rebirth/the-guhyasamaja-presentation-of-the-process-of-death?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Now return to phowa.

When a tantric text depicts consciousness transferring, the practitioner is not thereby entitled to assert the independent, substantial existence of a permanent traveller.

Within the tradition's conventional explanation, a **dependent mental continuum** is directed toward a different mode of existence.

From the standpoint of emptiness, neither the traveller, the process of travelling nor the destination possesses independent existence.

The whole process is empty.

But empty does not mean causally ineffective.

And suddenly phowa is much more interesting than shooting a mystical marble through your skull.

It's an attempt to alter the causal trajectory of a conscious continuum while understanding that continuum as lacking a permanent owner.

### But hang on. What about the clear-light mind?

Surely that survives?

Maybe it's the eternal bit?

Steady on.

In certain Highest Yoga Tantra systems, the subtlest level of mind is described as having a beginningless continuity and as continuing across lives.

But a beginningless mental continuum is not, for that reason, an inherently existing, changeless self.

In Gelug explanations, clear-light mental activity is still empty of self-established existence.

Even when that level of awareness is called *primordial*, one mustn't import the ontology of Śiva into the discussion without argument.

The difference matters enormously.

**Beginningless does not mean independent. Luminous does not mean substantial. Continuity does not mean an eternal person.**

In fact, the very insight meant to liberate the continuum involves seeing through the tendency to reify it.

The yogin who thinks they've found an indestructible personal observer at the heart of experience may, from a Madhyamaka perspective, have accidentally built themselves an extremely sophisticated new prison.

A transparent prison.

A beautifully decorated, blissfully luminous, non-conceptual prison.

But still a bloody prison.

## V. The five levels of phowa: from cosmic departure lounge to no departure at all

Now things become particularly elegant.

Not all phowa is equally literal in its understanding of transference.

In a classification transmitted by Patrul Rinpoche, five forms are distinguished: dharmakāya transference, sambhogakāya transference, nirmāṇakāya transference, ordinary transference through three recognitions, and transference performed for the deceased through compassion.

Other lineages classify these somewhat differently. This is a broader Tibetan presentation, not a claim that Patrul's fivefold scheme is the original sixfold arrangement of Nāropa. \[[Phowa](https://rigpawiki.org/index.php/Phowa?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Commentary on Transference of Consciousness](https://www.lotsawahouse.org/tibetan-masters/karma-chakme/phowa-commentary?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

### Ordinary phowa: consciousness has a destination

At the most accessible conceptual level, the practice has three principal components: a route, a traveller and a destination.

The route is the central channel.

The traveller is represented by consciousness.

The destination is a buddhafield.

The model makes sense because ordinary consciousness is so thoroughly organized around spatial relationships.

We know how to go somewhere.

We know how to leave somewhere.

We know how to imagine travelling toward a beloved figure.

So the practice uses these capacities.

Amitābha isn't merely functioning as a cosmic airport official processing the recently deceased.

The Buddha embodies immeasurable light, wisdom and compassionate activity. The aspiration for Sukhāvatī is directed toward a condition understood as especially conducive to awakening.

And this distinction matters: **rebirth in a pure land is not invariably identical with immediately attaining complete buddhahood**\.

In many presentations it is a supremely favorable environment for accomplishing the path.

Rather than another round of ordinary confusion, the practitioner aspires to conditions in which ignorance can finally be overcome.

There is also an unmistakably devotional dimension.

The practitioner doesn't simply operate an energetic mechanism. Faith, refuge, compassion, aspiration and identification with the guru and deity belong to the causal framework.

That's why phowa is not just esoteric plumbing.

The entire existential orientation is being redirected.

### Nirmāṇakāya phowa: return for the benefit of beings

Here the emphasis shifts toward enlightened manifestation and compassionate activity.

Why insist upon escape from embodied existence if one could appear in a form that benefits others?

The aim of the bodhisattva is not merely to secure a private, pleasant afterlife.

It is to overcome suffering and its causes for beings generally.

So the imagined destination need not be personal retirement.

Instead of "Get me the fuck out of saṃsāra," the aspiration becomes something more demanding.

"May the conditions of my existence be transformed so that I can actually help."

The metaphysical claim now involves not simply continuity, but the possibility of **a transformed mode of embodiment**\.

### Sambhogakāya phowa: dying into the awakened form

*Sambhogakāya* is conventionally the enjoyment body of a buddha, associated with exalted manifestations accessible to highly realized bodhisattvas.

In the phowa classifications, this level is associated with advanced deity yoga and the union of generation and completion.

The yogin does not merely think about a Buddha somewhere else.

The practices concern the transformation of the very mode in which embodied appearance is experienced.

Ordinary personal identity, subtle embodiment, deity appearance and the realization of emptiness are brought into relation.

The interesting thing isn't supernatural transport alone.

It's the increasing dissolution of the sharp distinction between an ordinary subject and an awakened form.

And then comes the final category.

### Dharmakāya phowa: the traveller disappears from the theory

This is the exquisite one.

In Karma Chakme's commentary on consciousness transference, available in translation at Lotsawa House, the highest transference concerns recognition of luminosity and is explicitly described as free of the ordinary conceptual distinction between the one transferring and what is transferred. \[[Commentary on Transference of Consciousness](https://www.lotsawahouse.org/tibetan-masters/karma-chakme/phowa-commentary?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Read that carefully.

At the most sophisticated level, the imagery of movement is transcended.

You aren't necessarily imagining a tiny person escaping through a cosmic skylight.

The decisive event is recognition of the nature of mind.

The mother and child luminosities meet.

The yogin recognizes the reality that ordinary appearances and confused identification previously concealed.

There is no substantial individual to send anywhere.

And here a rather beautiful reversal occurs.

**The highest phowa is no longer about relocating the individual. It is about the cessation of the fundamental misunderstanding that produces bondage.**

The literal-looking transference practice has reached a philosophical level at which its own central imagery is no longer ultimate.

The ladder has done its work.

You can stop dragging the ladder around.

This is also the point at which comparisons with Trika become most illuminating-and most treacherous.

Because Abhinavagupta is going to agree that ultimate liberation is not a journey through space.

But his explanation of what is recognized will be radically different.

## VI. Drong-jug: the absolutely deranged yoga of entering another body

Primary Tibetan source: Tilopa-attributed instructions, translated by Glenn Mullin. Later expositions: Tsongkhapa and modern Gelug presentations. Comparative precursor: Pātañjalayogaśāstra 3.38-39. \[[Readings On the Six Yogas of Naropa](https://www.shambhala.com/snowlion_articles/readings-on-the-six-yogas-of-naropa/?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Six Yogas of Naropa: Further Advanced Practices](https://studybuddhism.com/en/advanced-studies/vajrayana/tantra-advanced/introduction-to-the-six-yogas-of-naropa/six-yogas-of-naropa-further-advanced-practices?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Patañjali: Yogasūtra with Bhāṣya (= Pātañjalayogaśāstra) (GRETIL)](https://www.gretil.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_pataJjali-yogasUtra-with-bhASya.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Now we've reached the material that sounds as though somebody accidentally mixed an Indian tantric manuscript with a science-fiction novel.

*Grong 'jug*\.

Often translated as entering the citadel.

What citadel?

The bodily complex of another being.

The traditional claim is that a highly accomplished yogin may project their mental continuum into the body of someone recently deceased and make use of that embodiment.

Imagine the premises of the story.

You're an accomplished tantric practitioner.

Your existing body is no longer suitable for completing beneficial activity.

Another body has recently become available.

The yogic narrative proposes that its physical organization might be appropriated as a new vehicle for consciousness.

This isn't ordinary reincarnation.

It isn't merely the generation of a new birth through karmic conditions.

It is a claim about deliberately establishing consciousness in an already existing bodily structure.

A sort of metaphysical tenancy transfer.

Except the landlord is dead, the tenant is a mahāsiddha, and the letting agent is utterly beyond regulatory supervision.

There are important qualifications.

The classical presentation generally relates this accomplishment to exceptional realization and compassionate purposes. It is not supposed to be a casual method of obtaining a younger body because your knees hurt.

And there is no reliable scientific demonstration that a consciousness can be transferred into a corpse in this fashion.

The tradition records the claim. That is different from verifying the phenomenon.

### The extraordinary thing: India already had this idea

Consider Patañjali's *Yogasūtra* 3.38.

The text says\:

*bandhakāraṇaśaithilyāt ... cittasya paraśarīrāveśaḥ.*

The general meaning is that loosening the causes of bondage, together with awareness of the mind's movement, makes entering another body possible.

The accompanying classical commentary elaborates this in terms of the yogin's mind entering another body.

The immediately following sūtra, 3.39, connects mastery of the upward vital energy, *udāna*, with *utkrānti*, departure or ascent at death. \[[Patañjali: Yogasūtra with Bhāṣya (= Pātañjalayogaśāstra) (GRETIL)](https://www.gretil.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_pataJjali-yogasUtra-with-bhASya.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Now this is worth lingering over.

The relevant vocabulary and conceptual motifs existed in Indian traditions outside Vajrayāna.

They weren't invented wholesale in Tibet.

But be precise: Patañjali's Classical Yoga is not the Trika of Abhinavagupta. Nor does resemblance establish that a specific Buddhist passage was copied directly from this particular sūtra.

The broader Indian tantric environment contained multiple traditions exploring the relationship between subtle embodiment, vital energy, consciousness and deliberate departure.

Some were Buddhist.

Some were Śaiva.

Their exchanges and adaptations are a legitimate subject of historical research.

### The really difficult problem: what makes a body yours?

Let's get philosophical again.

We ordinarily assume that the relationship between consciousness and body is fixed.

This body is mine.

That body is yours.

The boundary appears metaphysically fundamental.

But what actually makes one body *mine*?

Is it causal continuity?

Memory?

Control?

The association between perception and action?

The maintenance of some subtle energetic organization?

Or is ownership itself an imputation based on a temporarily coherent set of processes?

The drong-jug literature radicalizes the problem.

If bodily identity were theoretically transferable, then the body could not be the ultimate criterion of personal identity.

But if there's no permanent self, the successful transfer could not involve a soul-substance moving from one container to another.

The tantric account would need to describe a reconfiguration of the conditions supporting a particular mental continuum.

The ridiculous-sounding miracle reveals a profoundly serious metaphysical question.

**Is the relation between consciousness and embodiment essential, or contingent?**

The drong-jug practitioner, according to the traditional theory, is attempting something far more radical than an out-of-body experience.

They're trying to demonstrate that one conscious continuity is not irrevocably tied to one physical organism.

Whether such a demonstration is possible remains unverified.

But the philosophical challenge is absolutely real.

And this is where Śaiva thought enters the room with an even bigger proposition.

What if *all* organisms are expressions of one universal consciousness?

## VII. Enter Abhinavagupta: you've misunderstood what you are

The metaphysics of consciousness in nondual Trika

Research basis: *Tantrasāra* 1-2, 15-16 and 19; *Paramārthasāra* 92-99 with Yogarāja's commentary; Loriliai Biernacki's scholarship on the subtle body; and the *Mālinīvijayottaratantra*\. \[[Abhinavagupta: Tantrasara](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/1_sanskr/6_sastra/3_phil/saiva/abhtantu.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Abhinavagupta: Paramārthasāra with Yogarāja's Paramārthasārasaṃgrahavivṛti commentary (GRETIL)](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_abhinavagupta-paramArthasAra-comm.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [The Subtle Body \| The Matter of Wonder: Abhinavagupta's Panentheism and the New Materialism](https://academic.oup.com/book/45384/chapter-abstract/389330272?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [TextGrid Repository](https://betaportal.textgrid.d.sub.uni-goettingen.de/browse/49ksb.0?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Right.

You've been exploring the possibility that consciousness might survive the loss of a body.

And Abhinavagupta is about to ask a question so aggressively nondual that the entire premise starts falling apart.

*Why did you assume consciousness was inside the body in the first place?*

Think carefully.

When you say consciousness is located in the head, what exactly are you claiming?

Certainly, your ordinary human experience depends on the functioning of the nervous system. Brain injury, anaesthesia, sleep and illness all affect the forms of experience available to you.

But Abhinavagupta's philosophical inquiry concerns a deeper question.

What is the nature of the very reality in which bodies, sensations, perceptions, spatial relations and thoughts are disclosed?

For Trika, the ultimate is *Paramaśiva*\.

Not merely a superhuman entity located somewhere beyond the universe.

Not a very large consciousness operating a celestial control room.

Rather, the absolute reality of consciousness and its inherent creative freedom.

Abhinavagupta understands consciousness as *prakāśa*, luminosity or self-disclosure, inseparable from *vimarśa*, its reflexive self-awareness.

Consciousness isn't simply a passive light illuminating objects.

It is dynamically self-revealing and self-articulating.

The world is not an alien substance standing outside that reality.

It is the manifestation of consciousness through its own *svātantrya*, freedom.

Abhinavagupta's system recognizes the reality of manifestation rather than reducing the world to sheer unreality. \[[Cosmic Process, Philosophy, and Soteriology in the Works of Abhinavagupta \| The Oxford Handbook of Tantric Studies](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/44001/chapter-abstract/425847172?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Kashmiri Shaiva Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/kashmiri/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Now here's the staggering implication.

You are not, ultimately, a little conscious thing trapped inside a biological machine, desperately hoping to escape through a hole in the roof.

Your deepest identity is the very consciousness within which the distinction between inside and outside is established.

The body appears.

The world appears.

Time appears.

Space appears.

Your sense of being a particular person appears.

And consciousness is never encountered as one more object alongside those appearances.

Now, be careful. This is Trika's metaphysical argument, not a conclusion experimentally established by neuroscience.

But within that argument, the usual account of consciousness transfer becomes philosophically inadequate.

How could universal consciousness leave one location and travel to another?

What location would be outside it?

Where could Śiva possibly go?

He's already there.

It's the cosmic equivalent of trying to move the ocean from one part of itself to another.

### But then why do I feel trapped inside my body?

Excellent question.

Now we reach *saṃkoca*\.

Contraction.

The unlimited, according to Trika, manifests itself as a limited subject.

The universal appears under the condition of particularity.

Instead of experiencing the fullness of consciousness, the individual experiences\:

"I am here."

"I am this body."

"I know these things, but not those things."

"I can do this, but cannot do that."

"I need something outside myself to become complete."

This condition is associated with the three *malas*, or limiting impurities.

*Āṇavamala* is the fundamental sense of incompleteness or limitation.

*Māyīyamala* is the condition in which subject and object appear divided into mutually external entities.

*Kārmamala* concerns the binding effects of limited agency and action.

These aren't literal dirty substances deposited on an otherwise clean soul.

They're technical explanations of how unlimited consciousness appears as a constrained experiencer.

And notice the delicious irony.

The ordinary individual thinks\:

"I am consciousness imprisoned in a body. I need to escape."

Trika responds\:

"The feeling that you are an imprisoned little consciousness is itself the contraction."

**Perhaps the most fundamental confinement is not the body but the identification that makes the body an absolute boundary.**

The problem is not solved merely by relocating that identification.

If you carry your limited self-conception into a heavenly realm, you've changed your circumstances without necessarily dissolving ignorance.

Congratulations. You've booked first class on the saṃsāra express.

Very comfortable seats.

Still saṃsāra.

## VIII. What actually reincarnates in Trika?

Now don't make the opposite mistake.

If universal consciousness never travels, does Abhinavagupta simply deny reincarnation?

Absolutely not.

This is where we need a distinction between **ultimate identity and the continuity of the contracted individual**\.

In Trika, the finite experiencer, the *jīva* or limited *pramātṛ*, is associated with a subtle psychophysical organization.

One important term is *puryaṣṭaka*\.

The eightfold city.

This is a subtle body, not simply a miniature replica of the gross physical organism.

It incorporates structures of limited cognition and vital functioning, allowing the finite subject's experience and karmic continuity to be explained.

Modern scholarship, including Loriliai Biernacki's work, examines how Abhinavagupta's subtle-body theory mediates between consciousness, agency, embodiment and transmigration. \[[The Subtle Body \| The Matter of Wonder: Abhinavagupta's Panentheism and the New Materialism](https://academic.oup.com/book/45384/chapter-abstract/389330272?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Karma and Rebirth in Hinduism](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/karma-and-rebirth-in-hinduism/93C67F86CB4CA592A37E8BBD96F18018?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Imagining the body in tantric contemplative practice \| International Journal of Dharma Studies](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40613-016-0043-7?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Here's the important distinction.

Śiva does not need to transmigrate.

But a finite manifestation of consciousness can remain conditioned by limitation and karma, undergoing successive forms of embodiment.

The physical body perishes.

The limited individuality, according to this cosmology, need not immediately dissolve into full recognition.

Its subtle continuity can support subsequent embodiment.

This allows Trika to explain why the death of a physical organism does not automatically end saṃsāra.

You could die with all your philosophical books neatly arranged, your Sanskrit impeccable, and your cosmic liberation still pending.

Extremely inconvenient.

### So does the subtle body physically travel somewhere?

Within the traditional cosmology, the finite being can undergo transitions between realms or forms of existence.

But the deepest ontological reality of all these processes remains Śiva.

Think of a succession of dream identities.

In one dream, you are a king.

In another, you're being pursued by an inexplicably hostile goose.

In another, you're a Tibetan yogin.

The dream personalities and worlds differ.

Yet from the standpoint of the dreaming subject, they are appearances within one experiential reality.

For Abhinavagupta, the analogy becomes cosmic, although his doctrine shouldn't be reduced to ordinary dream idealism.

The limited experiencer is real as a manifestation, but its limitation is not the ultimate truth of what it is.

Thus there are two questions.

What happens to the finite subject and its karmic dispositions when a body dies?

And what is the ultimate nature of the consciousness in which all such transitions occur?

The first admits cosmological answers involving subtle bodies and rebirth.

The second receives the answer of *Pratyabhijñā*\.

Recognition.

And this is why Trika is not simply a doctrine of escaping reincarnation through energetic manipulation.

The most fundamental liberation is the dissolution of mistaken self-identification through recognition of one's own Śiva-nature.

### Abhinavagupta gives an extraordinarily clear answer

Near the conclusion of the *Paramārthasāra*, verses 92-99, Abhinavagupta addresses precisely this territory.

He discusses the individual between bodies, acquiring another body, the persistence of realized knowledge through death, and what happens to an aspirant whose practice has not reached completion.

Verse 92 describes the individual associated with existence between bodies and its acquisition of another embodiment according to its condition.

Verse 93 says that the self's realization does not become something different merely through the body's fall.

Then verse 95 reaches the crucial point\:

*maraṇāvasare jñānī na cyavate svātmaparamārthāt.*

My translation\:

*At the moment of death, the knower does not fall away from the ultimate truth of their own Self.*

That is absolutely magnificent.

Because it is not primarily saying that the realized yogin performs an extraordinary manoeuvre at death.

It says that **death does not dislodge the realized knower from the truth already established**\.

The sensory faculties may fail.

Memory may fail.

The physical organism may undergo painful dissolution.

Yet the knowledge that constitutes realization is not thereby overturned.

This is a specifically Trika argument grounded in the distinction between bodily processes and the ultimate identity of the knower.

Verses 98-99 then consider someone who dies before full realization. Such a practitioner may attain an elevated condition and eventually become established in Śiva through a subsequent birth.

So there is no need to pretend that every aspirant is automatically liberated when the body expires.

The tradition preserves a genuine distinction between achieved realization and incomplete progress. \[[Abhinavagupta: Paramārthasāra (GRETIL)](https://www.gretil.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_abhinavagupta-paramArthasAra.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Abhinavagupta: Paramārthasāra with Yogarāja's Paramārthasārasaṃgrahavivṛti commentary (GRETIL)](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_abhinavagupta-paramArthasAra-comm.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

And now we are ready for the astonishing ritual material.

Because the very same Abhinavagupta who teaches recognition beyond the body's limitations also describes highly technical interventions at death.

No contradiction has yet been established.

But there's definitely a philosophical problem worth examining.

## IX. Trika's own death yoga: Śaiva utkrānti

*Utkrānti* refers to departure, ascent or going forth.

Its use for deliberate departure from the body is attested in Śaiva tantric traditions, notably in sources such as the *Mālinīvijayottaratantra* and *Kubjikāmatatantra*\.

The broader history reaches beyond Abhinavagupta and overlaps with older South Asian ideas of bodily exit-points and postmortem destinations.

This isn't a Tibetan monopoly on spectacular death rituals. \[[Tracing the Roots of Samādhi Burial Practice \| The Journal of Hindu Studies](https://academic.oup.com/jhs/article/14/1/8/6316037?guestAccessKey=4a79041d-019c-4b68-a9cf-6b155b58563d&utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Lord in the Temple, Lord in the Tomb: The Hindu Temple and Its Relationship to the Samādhi Shrine Tradition of Jnāneśvar Mahārāj](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/4650q3zk?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [The Yoga of the Mālinivijayottaratantra. Chapters 1–4, 7, 12–17.](https://www.ifpindia.org/bookstore/ci97/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

But there are distinctions among Śaiva *utkrānti*, initiation at death, postmortem initiation, and liberating cremation.

They are related practices, not interchangeable names.

### Tantrasāra Chapter 15: liberation at the brink of death

Now listen carefully, because this is one of our most important primary-text discoveries.

Abhinavagupta's *Tantrasāra* Chapter 15 describes *sadyaḥ samutkramaṇadīkṣā*\.

An initiation for immediate departure or ascent.

The setting is a disciple whose death is approaching and for whom an appropriate descent of divine power, *śaktipāta*, has manifested.

The teacher performs a liberating initiation.

The ritual involves the purification of the pathways of manifestation, the invocation of Kālarātrī, the symbolic severing of bonds associated with vital points, and the placement of the disciple's consciousness at the crown aperture.

The consummation is the ritual unification of the departing individual with Paramaśiva.

This isn't my imaginative reconstruction. It is what *Tantrasāra* 15.1-3 actually describes. \[[Abhinavagupta: Tantrasara](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/1_sanskr/6_sastra/3_phil/saiva/abhtantu.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Let's examine what this means.

The disciple isn't simply being sent to a more comfortable universe.

The intended outcome is identity with Paramaśiva.

And the operative structure is not exclusively the disciple's ability to manipulate internal energies.

It is also *dīkṣā*, initiation, mediated through the guru and grounded in divine grace.

The ritual is conceived as effecting a transformation in the disciple's bondage.

Now compare that with phowa.

Both systems employ an extraordinary vocabulary of movement, ascent and a privileged upper aperture.

Both consider death a moment when the ordinary relation between embodiment and consciousness can be transformed.

Both describe the possibility of deliberate intervention.

But they interpret the intervention within different metaphysical and soteriological systems.

For the Tibetan practitioner, the outcome may be rebirth in a buddhafield or the recognition of dharmakāya.

For the Trika initiate, the ultimate intended outcome is recognition or realization of identity with Śiva.

Those aren't simply alternative words for the same thing.

And the person administering the ritual matters.

A Śaiva guru is not merely acting as a technician.

Within the tradition's theology, the guru can function as an instrument of Śiva's liberating power.

### Tantrasāra Chapter 16: the guru can supposedly rescue the dead

Now the material gets extraordinarily wild.

Chapter 16 concerns initiation in the absence of the disciple, including a disciple who has already died.

Abhinavagupta describes ritual methods for drawing the departed individual into a substitute ritual embodiment, performing initiation, and effecting liberation.

The substitute may be a figure made from ritual materials.

The ritual involves a method called the *mahājāla*, the great net.

In the text's traditional account, the guru draws the departed being into the ritual context and accomplishes the appropriate purificatory transformation.

Abhinavagupta even distinguishes consequences depending upon the condition in which the deceased is understood to exist.

In certain circumstances liberation is effected; in others the outcome is access to further knowledge, yoga or initiation.

That's an important nuance.

The text doesn't simply treat every departed being as identical in karmic circumstance or ritual eligibility. \[[Abhinavagupta: Tantrasara](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/1_sanskr/6_sastra/3_phil/saiva/abhtantu.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Now think about the metaphysical assumptions.

The deceased individual has a continuity beyond the loss of the visible body.

That continuity is accessible to divine intervention.

The effects of karma are not necessarily beyond transformation.

And the relation between liberated guru, initiation, cosmic powers and individual destiny is not constrained by ordinary bodily proximity.

Within the ritual imagination, the guru's activity can extend beyond death.

That is a considerably stronger claim than merely saying that a funeral helps grieving relatives psychologically.

But again, we're describing the claim made by the text, not confirming its supernatural efficacy.

### Tantrasāra Chapter 19: funeral initiation and the utterly astonishing final qualification

Chapter 19 deals with further rites for the deceased.

It describes the ritual manipulation and purification of the departed person's relation to bondage.

The Sanskrit even includes the striking assertion that, through the prescribed vital-energy ritual, the corpse trembles.

The resulting transformation is understood through the joining of the finite being to Paramaśiva. \[[Abhinavagupta: Tantrasara](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/1_sanskr/6_sastra/3_phil/saiva/abhtantu.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Imagine reading that without knowing the source.

You'd understandably think someone had written a particularly unhinged occult manual.

Yet here it is in Abhinavagupta.

But then comes the sentence that changes how we should read the entire bloody system.

*Tantrasāra* 19.6 states that these funerary rites are not useful for the *tattvajñānin*\.

The knower of reality.

In other words, **one who has realized the truth does not require these postmortem ritual interventions**\.

Abhinavagupta even understands the realized knower's death as an occasion of significance for those continuing the transmission of knowledge. \[[Abhinavagupta: Tantrasara](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/1_sanskr/6_sastra/3_phil/saiva/abhtantu.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

There it is.

The hierarchy.

The elaborate ritual techniques possess meaning and validity within the tradition.

But they do not constitute an ultimate necessity for the one established in realization.

It's almost comically beautiful.

The same text that offers you an entire metaphysical emergency service for dying and dead people eventually says\:

"If you've actually realized the truth, none of this is required."

The ritual spaceship is available.

But the liberated one doesn't need transport.

## X. Can one consciousness enter another body in Trika?

This question deserves precision because it's where careless comparisons become misleading.

Indian religious literature contains a substantial collection of ideas concerning *parakāyapraveśa*, entry into another's body, and *paraśarīrāveśa*, the entrance of the mind into another body.

As we have seen, the Pātañjalayogaśāstra provides an early and explicit textual precedent.

Śaiva tantric literature also includes accounts of divine possession, yogic entry, subtle embodiment and powers associated with advanced practice.

Abhinavagupta's account of *āveśa* and *samāveśa*, however, must not automatically be translated as the literal transfer of one person's consciousness into another corpse.

Often it concerns immersion in, or possession by, divine consciousness.

That is not the same ritual phenomenon as drong-jug.

We would be manufacturing textual equivalence if we treated every instance of *praveśa* as proof of the same practice. \[[देवेषु विश्वासटिप्पन्यः](https://vishvasa.github.io/devaH/meta/AveshaH/articles/self-possessed/4_Worldly_and_Otherworldly_Ruptures/10_Possession_in_Tantra/02_Discipline_and_Enlightenment/?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Kashmiri Shaiva Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/kashmiri/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

But there is an extraordinary philosophical comparison available.

### The Buddhist question

Can a mental continuum establish a new relation to physical embodiment?

### The Trika question

Can universal consciousness manifest itself as different centers of finite experience?

These aren't identical questions.

Buddhism begins from dependently arisen mental processes and rejects an independent self.

Trika begins from the absolute freedom of consciousness and explains particular subjects as contractions of its manifestation.

And here is the difference.

In the Buddhist account, the challenge is how causal continuity can obtain without a persisting self-substance.

In Trika, the challenge is how a universal reality can manifest finite, apparently separate subjects without ceasing to be one.

Two distinct metaphysical problems.

One asks how continuity works without identity of substance.

The other asks how plurality works without ultimate separation.

**Both destabilize the ordinary assumption that an independently existing person simply inhabits an independently existing body.**

But they destabilize it in very different ways.

The Buddhist analyses the person into dependence.

The Trika philosopher recognizes the finite person as a limited manifestation of absolute consciousness.

This is why the superficial claim that Tibetan Buddhism and Kashmir Śaivism teach exactly the same thing cannot survive serious examination.

They can make use of strikingly similar ritual technologies while disagreeing about the ultimate reality those technologies are supposed to reveal.

## XI. The great showdown: emptiness versus the universal I

Now we have arrived at the philosophical summit.

Put Nāropa's Buddhist inheritance on one side.

Put Abhinavagupta's Pratyabhijñā on the other.

Ask them both\:

"What is the ultimate nature of consciousness?"

And prepare for an argument that no amount of incense will resolve.

### The Buddhist position

The most subtle consciousness, however extraordinary, cannot be established as an intrinsically existing Self.

Its nature is emptiness.

Its appearances arise dependently.

In the Madhyamaka philosophical framework, no phenomenon-including mind-possesses independent self-existence.

The ultimate realization does not consist in discovering a permanent metaphysical subject behind phenomena.

It consists in wisdom realizing the emptiness of such supposed independent identities.

But do not misunderstand this as the total extinction of awareness.

The Mahāyāna and tantric discussions of the awakened mind, luminosity and buddhahood are considerably more sophisticated than a crude philosophy of nothingness.

Emptiness is not nihilism.

Neither is luminosity a disguised eternal soul.

### The Trika position

The ultimate reality is not merely an absence of inherent existence.

It is positively articulated as self-revealing, self-aware consciousness.

*Prakāśa-vimarśa.*

The inseparable luminosity and reflexive awareness of Śiva.

All manifestation is understood as an expression of this divine consciousness.

The highest recognition is not simply that the individual has no independently existing essence.

It is that the individual's ultimate nature is the absolute, which has manifested itself in finite form.

The realized *aham* is not the empirical ego.

It is the universal *I*\.

The difference is crucial.

The everyday ego says, "I am this particular person."

The universal *aham* is the reflexive self-awareness of the totality of consciousness, not a gigantic human personality.

Abhinavagupta's *Tantrasāra* Chapter 2 expresses the highest standpoint through the identity of the self with the unbounded reality of consciousness. \[[Abhinavagupta: Tantrasara](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/1_sanskr/6_sastra/3_phil/saiva/abhtantu.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Now compare what happens to the idea of transferring consciousness.

For the Buddhist practitioner, ultimate insight reveals that the supposed traveller cannot be found as an independent entity.

For the Trika practitioner, ultimate insight reveals that the true Self was never limited to the apparent traveller.

Both can lead to a cessation of the ordinary compulsion to secure the limited individual.

But they're not the same philosophical result.

The Buddhist does not have to affirm an eternal universal Self.

The Śaiva does.

That disagreement cannot be erased by pointing out that both talk about luminous awareness.

### Then why are their highest practices so remarkably similar?

This is the genuinely interesting comparative question.

There may be similarities in meditative phenomenology: the quieting of conceptualization, loss of ordinary bodily identification, altered experience of agency, and the disclosure of awareness apart from its usual objects.

There are also historical connections between Buddhist and Śaiva tantric ritual cultures.

Kris Anderson's research on Buddhist funerary *utkrānti* traces meaningful parallels with Śaiva rites and discusses the probable adoption and transformation of related techniques in Buddhist tantric literature.

The historical relationship is more interesting than a simple assertion that one tradition copied the other wholesale. \[[UC Berkeley](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4zr3j0m9/qt4zr3j0m9.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

A shared contemplative or ritual architecture need not generate identical philosophical interpretations.

The same human experience can be theorized in different ways.

A state of non-conceptual luminosity doesn't arrive carrying a certificate that says "Madhyamaka" or "Trika".

Interpretation matters.

Training matters.

The prior conceptual framework matters.

And this gives us a remarkable possibility.

Different traditions may develop practices that transform similar structures of ordinary experience while interpreting the results through substantially different ontologies.

One might describe the result through emptiness and dependent origination.

Another through Śiva's self-recognition and freedom.

Whether these interpretations ultimately refer to the same realized condition cannot be established merely by noticing vocabulary in common.

That question demands philosophical argument, phenomenological care and textual precision.

## XII. The final absurdity: you were trying to escape yourself

Now imagine two accomplished practitioners approaching death.

One is a Tibetan Vajrayāna yogin trained in the Six Dharmas.

The other is a Trika adept established in recognition.

We won't pretend to know their subjective experience or metaphysical destiny.

Instead, consider the ideals articulated by their traditions.

The Buddhist practitioner has cultivated familiarity with the nature of appearances, the subtle dimensions of mind, and the possibility of recognizing emptiness as ordinary cognition dissolves.

If sufficiently realized, the practitioner need not treat death as a catastrophic interruption of the path.

The highest aspiration is not mere continuation of personal consciousness.

It is liberation from ignorance and the capacity for awakened activity benefiting beings.

The Trika adept has realized that the ultimate Self is not a finite subject confined to an organism.

The physical body's destruction does not entail destruction of the consciousness understood as its absolute reality.

For one established in that knowledge, the process of dying is not fundamentally a journey from one place to another.

It is another event within the freedom of Śiva's manifestation.

And now remember the poor bastard at the beginning of this reading.

Terrified of losing himself.

Desperately researching how consciousness might survive.

Imagining spiritual tunnels, luminous realms and exotic bodily exits.

"What happens to me?"

"Where will I go?"

"Can I direct the process?"

"What if I do it wrong?"

The Buddhist asks whether the self being defended was ever independently established.

The Trika philosopher asks whether the Self was ever confined to the thing being defended.

And they both, in their different ways, undermine the original panic.

Not by providing a certificate of immortality.

Not by promising that a particular technique will function like an emergency escape hatch.

But by examining the structure of identification itself.

### The question beneath phowa

Try asking this, not as a deathbed technique, but as an investigation of present experience.

What is it that I am afraid will disappear?

My memories?

My ability to act?

My relationships?

My bodily sensations?

My sense of being a particular person?

And is the fear of losing these things evidence that there is an independent owner behind them?

Or is that very ownership something constructed within experience?

Buddhist inquiry may investigate the dependent arising of that ownership and the possibility of relinquishing grasping.

Trika may investigate the contraction through which universal consciousness identifies itself as a limited subject.

Neither inquiry requires pretending that ordinary death is harmless, that grief is ignorance, or that biology does not matter.

And neither should become an excuse to chase dangerous experiments in breath retention or premature departure from life.

The value of these teachings isn't dependent on attempting to reproduce supernatural claims.

It is already present in the philosophical question of what constitutes selfhood.

### And Abhinavagupta delivers the final blow

Return once more to *Paramārthasāra* 95\.

The knower does not fall from the truth of the Self at death.

In *Tantrasāra* 19.6, the realized knower does not require the postmortem rites.

The implication is absolutely beautiful.

The point of realization is not that you have finally acquired the most powerful method of escaping a body.

It is that the fundamental truth of what you are is no longer understood as dependent upon acquiring a new location.

The Tibetan Buddhist version, at the highest level of dharmakāya transference, similarly transcends the literal duality of a separate traveller and destination.

But its philosophical justification remains grounded in Buddhist realization, not the affirmation of Śiva as universal Self.

At the surface, the traditions appear to propose an almost impossibly elaborate cosmic transport system.

At their highest levels, the literal idea of transport becomes increasingly secondary.

The Buddhist dismantles the imaginary owner of experience.

The Trika adept recognizes the unlimited consciousness that was appearing as a limited owner.

And both force the practitioner to confront the same devastatingly simple question\:

**What exactly were you trying to save?**

Perhaps the strangest thing about these systems is that they begin with the fear of death and end by undermining the assumptions that make death appear to threaten an independently existing self.

The ordinary person imagines liberation as getting away from something.

The Tibetan yogin investigates the empty nature of the one who would get away.

The Śaiva investigates the contraction through which the infinite appears to need escape.

And at that point, the entire cosmic departure lounge looks rather different.

Not because we've proved what survives death.

But because we've finally started questioning who was supposed to board the flight.
