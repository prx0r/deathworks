# REBIRTH: THE MOST TERRIFYING POSSIBILITY IN EXISTENCE

The Final Reading - Trika Śaivism, Tibetan Buddhism, the Machinery of Saṃsāra, and Why Death Might Not Be the End of Anything

*What is rebirth? Why does it happen? Who gets reborn? What actually carries karma? Why doesn't the modern West believe it? And what if the most consequential fact about your existence is one you've spent your whole life ignoring?*

---

## I. The universe might not be letting you off that easily.

Right, you beautiful, existentially confused bastard.

I've got some potentially catastrophic news.

You might not get to die.

Now, obviously, your body is going to die. That's fairly well established. Your heart will stop, your brain will eventually cease functioning, your possessions will be distributed, and somebody will have to decide what to do with the approximately seventeen thousand unfinished projects on your computer.

But there's a possibility that two of the most sophisticated contemplative traditions in human history have spent centuries trying to articulate.

**What if the death of your present organism is not the end of the causal process that produced your experience of being someone?**

Not the survival of your personality exactly.

Not an immortal Victorian gentleman floating around with his memories, waistcoat and preferred brand of tobacco.

Something considerably stranger.

What if the process that makes experience arise continues under different conditions?

What if your sense of being *this* particular human being is a temporary organization of a much deeper continuity?

What if the profound conviction that this life is all there is turns out to be not knowledge, but an assumption?

And what if the seemingly innocent things you do every day-wanting, fearing, grasping, resenting, identifying, craving-are not merely psychological episodes?

What if they're participating in the conditions for another existence?

Now the stakes have become enormous.

And we are not going to resolve them by confidently declaring that ancient Indians knew everything or that modern neuroscientists have already explained away every difficult question about consciousness.

Neither position is justified.

We're going to investigate the actual metaphysical mechanisms, the historical texts, and the evidence.

Because the question is not simply whether reincarnation sounds attractive.

The question is whether the ordinary assumption that consciousness must necessarily end with the organism has been established-and whether the alternative accounts can withstand serious scrutiny.

Let's start with a distinction that changes everything.

## II. Rebirth is not necessarily reincarnation

In ordinary English, people use *rebirth* and *reincarnation* interchangeably.

But Buddhist and Śaiva philosophers are not necessarily claiming the same thing.

TRIKA ŚAIVISM

**The contracted subject takes another embodiment.**

Ultimate reality is Śiva, universal self-aware consciousness. A limited individual, conditioned by ignorance, karma and a subtle body, undergoes successive births.

---

TIBETAN BUDDHISM

**A conditioned continuum generates another life.**

No permanent self transmigrates. Consciousness and karmic dispositions continue dependently, giving rise to another set of psychophysical processes.

Both traditions distinguish ordinary karmic rebirth from the freedom of the realized practitioner, but disagree about what consciousness ultimately is. \[[Karma and Rebirth in Hinduism](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/karma-and-rebirth-in-hinduism/93C67F86CB4CA592A37E8BBD96F18018?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Statement of His Holiness the Fourteenth Dalai Lama, Tenzin Gyatso, on the Issue of His Reincarnation](https://tibetoffice.org/media-press/featured-news/statement-of-his-holiness-the-fourteenth-dalai-lama-tenzin-gyatso-on-the-issue-of-his-reincarnation?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Buddha](https://iep.utm.edu/buddha/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

That distinction is absolutely essential.

In Buddhism, the claim is not that your eternal soul leaves your corpse, wanders around the universe and takes up residence in a newborn.

Indeed, the Buddha criticized precisely the theory of an identical consciousness wandering unchanged through successive lives.

In Trika, by contrast, universal consciousness is affirmed as the ultimate Self. Yet even here, it isn't quite right to say that Śiva physically migrates between bodies.

Śiva was never confined to the first body.

**The Buddhist asks how continuity can exist without a permanent self. The Śaiva asks how the universal Self can experience itself as a succession of limited beings.**

Two staggeringly difficult questions.

And they lead to two different explanations for the same appalling predicament.

You are born.

You become somebody.

You desperately try to remain that somebody.

You die.

And, according to both traditions, the conditions of that desperate identification may not yet be exhausted.

So the whole business happens again.

Welcome to saṃsāra.

The subscription nobody remembers purchasing.

And, according to these traditions, the cancellation policy requires enlightenment.

---

## III. Trika: why the fuck does Śiva become an ignorant person?

Here's where the metaphysics gets genuinely extraordinary.

In Abhinavagupta's system, reality is not fundamentally composed of independent material objects with consciousness mysteriously emerging somewhere along the way.

The absolute is *Paramaśiva*\: consciousness inseparable from its capacity to know itself and manifest an entire world.

Its essential freedom is *svātantrya*\.

And that freedom includes the ability to manifest itself as apparently limited.

Now consider the implications.

Śiva is unlimited.

But an unlimited reality that could never appear limited would, in one sense, be constrained.

Its freedom would have an exception.

So the tradition understands the manifestation of finite subjects as an expression of divine freedom.

**The unlimited manifests the experience of limitation without ultimately ceasing to be unlimited.**

That's the masterstroke of Trika.

And also the beginning of an absolutely enormous philosophical headache.

Because if Śiva freely manifests limitation, does that mean Śiva is responsible for ignorance, suffering and rebirth?

In the ultimate metaphysical account, the manifestation of bondage belongs to Śiva's own power. The tradition doesn't posit a second, independent cosmic villain responsible for corrupting an otherwise helpless God.

Abhinavagupta's *Paramārthasāra* and Yogarāja's commentary explicitly describe the universe as the self-manifestation and self-concealment of divine consciousness. \[[Abhinavagupta: Paramārthasāra with Yogarāja's Paramārthasārasaṃgrahavivṛti commentary (GRETIL)](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_abhinavagupta-paramArthasAra-comm.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Kāşmir śaivism](https://ebooks.inflibnet.ac.in/icp03/chapter/kasmir-saivism/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

### The original cosmic identity crisis

Imagine consciousness that is not limited to one particular viewpoint.

Not one person's thinking.

Not a gigantic human brain.

The very reality within which all knowing and experiencing is possible.

According to Trika, consciousness freely manifests differentiated viewpoints.

And a viewpoint necessarily involves limitation.

To experience *this*, rather than *everything*, involves a particular perspective.

To act as *this individual*, rather than as universal agency, involves specific capacities.

To know a particular object involves the distinction between knower and known.

The absolute appears as the finite.

The cosmic *aham*, the universal I, appears as\:

"Hello. I'm a particular person. I'm approximately six feet tall, have unresolved emotional issues, and would really prefer it if reality started behaving according to my expectations."

Congratulations.

Śiva has invented anxiety.

But this is not meant to suggest that the divine literally suffers from human psychological confusion in the same way as a limited person.

The philosophical point is that finite subjectivity is a real mode of manifestation without being the ultimate identity of consciousness.

And the process of assuming limitation is called *saṃkoca*\.

Contraction.

### The three locks on the prison

Trika explains the finite individual's condition through three *malas*\.

1

**Āṇavamala - I am small.**

The fundamental contraction: I am incomplete, limited, insufficient. I do not possess the fullness of being, knowledge or agency.

---

2

**Māyīyamala - everything is separate.**

The differentiation of experience into apparently independent subjects and objects. I am here; fulfillment is somewhere else.

---

3

**Kārmamala - I act, acquire consequences, and remain bound.**

Limited agency generates the karmic conditions for further experience and embodiment.

\[[Abhinavagupta: Paramārthasāra with Yogarāja's Paramārthasārasaṃgrahavivṛti commentary (GRETIL)](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_abhinavagupta-paramArthasAra-comm.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Karma and Rebirth in Hinduism](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/karma-and-rebirth-in-hinduism/93C67F86CB4CA592A37E8BBD96F18018?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Now watch the logic.

First, consciousness experiences itself as incomplete.

Then, the world appears as something outside it.

Now the individual seeks completion through objects, relationships, possessions, status, sensory experience, achievement or control.

It acts.

Those actions leave consequences and dispositions.

The dispositions reinforce the limited subject's tendencies.

Further experiences arise.

More actions occur.

And the structure perpetuates itself.

**The individual doesn't merely undergo saṃsāra. Its mistaken identification helps perpetuate the conditions of saṃsāra.**

This is more than ordinary psychological repetition, though the latter provides an analogy.

The tradition explicitly extends this pattern beyond the death of the gross body.

### Abhinavagupta says it in an actual verse

*Paramārthasāra*, verse 54\:

**लोकव्यवहारकृतां य इहाविद्यामुपासते मूढाः।
ते यान्ति जन्ममृत्यू धर्माधर्मार्गलाबद्धाः॥**

*lokavyavahārakṛtāṃ ya ihāvidyām upāsate mūḍhāḥ &#124;
te yānti janmamṛtyū dharmādharmārgalābaddhāḥ &#124;&#124;*

My translation\:

"Those deluded ones who cultivate ignorance shaped by ordinary worldly dealings undergo birth and death, bound by the bolts of merit and demerit."

Yogarāja's commentary is explicit: these are individuals identifying themselves with their bodies, seeking the fruits of action and remaining subject to recurring birth and death. \[[Abhinavagupta: Paramārthasāra with Yogarāja's Paramārthasārasaṃgrahavivṛti commentary (GRETIL)](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_abhinavagupta-paramArthasAra-comm.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Here's the extraordinary detail.

**Both good karma and bad karma can bind.**

What?

Good karma too?

Yes.

In this account, meritorious action may produce desirable experiences and unfavorable action undesirable ones. But insofar as the individual continues to appropriate action and its results through limited identity, both belong to the machinery of conditioned existence.

Imagine spending a hundred lifetimes becoming extraordinarily virtuous in order to accumulate better heavenly accommodation.

You've made considerable progress.

The celestial real estate is fantastic.

But if your identity still depends upon being a finite enjoyer of its karmic rewards, the fundamental contraction remains.

Trika's liberation is not simply becoming the universe's most successful customer.

It is overcoming the ignorance that makes consciousness imagine itself essentially incomplete.

### So what actually survives the body?

Now we're ready for the mechanism.

Abhinavagupta and later commentators distinguish universal consciousness from the limited individual associated with a subtle body, the *puryaṣṭaka*\.

In the relevant Śaiva account, this eightfold organization involves vital functions, sensory and active capacities, and the inner cognitive apparatus.

It is not a scientifically identified anatomical structure. It is a metaphysical explanation for the continuing organization of finite experience. \[[Karma and Rebirth in Hinduism](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/karma-and-rebirth-in-hinduism/93C67F86CB4CA592A37E8BBD96F18018?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

The physical organism dies.

The karmic dispositions have not necessarily been exhausted.

The limited experiencer continues according to the tradition's subtle-body account.

And the conditions of the next embodiment reflect the accumulated tendencies and karmic consequences of the individual.

Now listen to *Paramārthasāra* 92\:

**स्वर्गमयो निरयमयस्तदयं देहान्तरालगः पुरुषः।
तद्भङ्गे स्वौचित्याद्देहान्तरयोगमभ्येति॥**

*svargamayo nirayamayastadayaṃ dehāntarālagaḥ puruṣaḥ &#124;
tadbhaṅge svaucityāddehāntarayogam abhyeti &#124;&#124;*

My translation\:

"This person, dwelling in the interval between bodies and conditioned toward heaven or hell, upon that body's dissolution enters into connection with another body according to what is appropriate to their condition."

Yogarāja explains that the relevant appropriateness concerns dispositions produced by previous action, directing the individual toward another embodiment suitable for the experience of its karmic results. \[[Abhinavagupta: Paramārthasāra with Yogarāja's Paramārthasārasaṃgrahavivṛti commentary (GRETIL)](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_abhinavagupta-paramArthasAra-comm.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

So the sequence in Trika is approximately\:

**Self-contraction**

I am a limited, separate experiencer

**Desire, identification and limited action**

Karmic dispositions are established

**Death of the gross body**

The subtle continuity is not necessarily released from bondage

**Another embodiment**

Conditions arise for further karmic experience

Conceptual reconstruction of Trika's causal account, not an observed biological mechanism.

Now consider the genuinely astonishing consequence.

You may not remember your previous life because autobiographical memory belongs to a particular cognitive and bodily organization.

Yet, according to the tradition, a deeper continuity of disposition and karmic conditioning may persist.

The next person doesn't have to possess your present personality.

The system does not require all your present memories, beliefs and preferences to remain intact.

What persists is a conditioned individual continuity-not necessarily the psychological autobiography you ordinarily call yourself.

And now the genuinely devastating question appears.

**If you don't remember the previous person, in what sense is the next life yours?**

Trika has an answer: the continuity concerns the contracted experiencer and its karmic organization, not the persistence of the same gross personality.

But that is a metaphysical explanation. It remains to be established that such a continuity exists beyond bodily death.

And it doesn't resolve every philosophical problem concerning personal identity.

It gives the tradition's answer to what travels.

We still need to understand why the whole process ever began.

## IV. But WHY did Śiva create this enormous suffering machine?

Now we've reached the hardest question in Trika.

Not how rebirth operates.

Why there is a universe involving bondage in the first place.

The tradition answers through *svātantrya*, divine freedom, and through the language of *krīḍā*, play.

Śiva manifests an endlessly differentiated universe through his own freedom.

There is no second independent substance from which the world must be manufactured.

And the absolute isn't motivated by lack in the way a finite person is.

A perfect being doesn't need entertainment because it's bored.

So divine play is not supposed to mean\:

"Tuesday was a bit quiet, so Śiva invented cancer, jealousy, famine and several trillion frightened animals."

That would be an absolutely horrifying theology.

The deeper explanation is that manifestation belongs to consciousness's inherent freedom.

Differentiation is possible because the absolute is dynamically creative, not a static, powerless substance.

But here's the objection that cannot simply be laughed away.

If Śiva possesses unlimited freedom, why manifest such extraordinary suffering?

Why not create a universe in which conscious beings enjoy unlimited freedom without undergoing the horrors of ignorance?

Calling something *play* does not, by itself, justify suffering.

And the Trika answer, powerful as it is, does not settle this objection to everyone's satisfaction.

It offers a nondual account in which bondage is not ultimately independent of Śiva, and liberation is the recovery of one's true identity.

It does not deliver a universally compelling moral explanation for every instance of agony.

That matters. A doctrine of divine self-concealment must never become an excuse to minimize another being's suffering.

### The distinction that saves the metaphysics from complete nonsense

In Trika, Śiva's self-concealment isn't necessarily imagined as a single historical event that occurred on the universe's first Tuesday.

Divine freedom is not itself bound by the temporal processes it manifests.

The question "What happened immediately before Śiva became ignorant?" may therefore import an inappropriate temporal assumption.

The finite individual has a causal history.

The absolute is not understood as beginning at a particular moment within that history.

So Trika distinguishes the ultimate ground of manifestation from the chronological sequence of karmic lives.

**Ultimately, rebirth is possible because Śiva freely manifests finite experience. Conventionally, rebirth continues because finite experience remains bound by ignorance and karma.**

That's the deepest Trika explanation.

Not one cause, but two levels of explanation.

Why is there a world in which limited subjects can exist?

Divine manifestation.

Why does a particular limited subject undergo another birth?

Karmic bondage.

And how does liberation occur?

Through the ending of ignorance and the recognition of the Self, within the tradition's account of grace and spiritual practice.

Which leads to Abhinavagupta's final twist.

### You do not need to die to become free

*Paramārthasāra* 55 compares accumulated karma to combustible material destroyed by the fire of knowledge.

Verse 56 argues that actions performed in the state of realization do not produce the same binding consequences.

Verses 85-86 explain why the liberated person may continue living in a body without the body becoming a cause of renewed saṃsāra. \[[Abhinavagupta: Paramārthasāra with Yogarāja's Paramārthasārasaṃgrahavivṛti commentary (GRETIL)](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_abhinavagupta-paramArthasAra-comm.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Abhinavagupta: Paramārthasāra with Yogarāja's Paramārthasārasaṃgrahavivṛti commentary (GRETIL)](https://gretil.sub.uni-goettingen.de/gretil/corpustei/transformations/html/sa_abhinavagupta-paramArthasAra-comm.htm?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Think about that.

The organism can continue.

Perception can continue.

Speech can continue.

Action can continue.

But the relationship to them changes fundamentally.

Liberation isn't achieved by murdering the personality or abandoning the body.

It's recognition that the limited identity was never the total truth of consciousness.

The cosmic actor realizes the part is a part.

The performance can continue.

But the fundamental confusion has ended.

## V. Tibetan Buddhism: the cosmic machinery runs on ignorance

Now enter the Buddhist.

And immediately, we encounter a rather spectacular disagreement.

The Trika philosopher has explained the universe through Śiva's self-concealing freedom.

The Buddhist says\:

"Interesting. Now demonstrate that this universal Self exists."

No divine creator is required.

No supreme consciousness needs to manifest itself as billions of confused individuals.

Instead, Tibetan Buddhism inherits the Buddhist account of *pratītyasamutpāda*\.

Dependent origination.

And the fundamental principle is devastatingly simple.

**When the appropriate conditions exist, something arises. When those conditions cease, the corresponding result ceases.**

In the theory of rebirth, this principle applies to the existence of the individual itself.

Not merely to what the individual experiences.

To the actual production and continuation of embodied sentient existence.

### 5.1 The twelve links: how you manufacture another life

The traditional twelvefold account can be presented as a causal sequence\:

THE CAUSES

**Ignorance - avidyā**  **Karmic formations**

Misunderstanding reality conditions intentional action and karmic tendencies.

THE ARISING OF EXPERIENCE

**Consciousness → Name-and-form → Six sense bases → Contact → Feeling**

A conditioned psychophysical life develops, perceives and feels.

THE REACTIVATION OF BONDAGE

**Craving → Clinging → Becoming**

Experience is appropriated; karmic potential for further existence is activated.

THE RESULT

**Birth → Aging and death**

Another conditioned life unfolds, sustaining the possibility of another cycle.

Traditional twelve-link model. The links have several exegetical arrangements, often spanning three lifetimes; the diagram is not meant to imply twelve successive physical events within a few seconds. \[[Buddha](https://iep.utm.edu/buddha/?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [The meaning of existence (bhava) in the Pāli discourses of the Buddha](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9662180/?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Reflection On The Twelve Links of Dependent-Origination](https://www.pathgate.org/index.php/gateway-to-tibetan-buddhism/567-reflection-on-the-twelve-links-of-dependent-origination?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Now look at the middle of this sequence.

*Feeling.*

*Craving.*

*Clinging.*

That's where something extraordinary happens.

You encounter an experience.

It is pleasant.

You want more.

It is unpleasant.

You want it to stop.

It is neutral.

You barely notice it.

Then the mind adds something.

"I need this."

"I cannot bear that."

"This belongs to me."

"This is what I am."

The experience has become incorporated into the construction of a self.

And that construction motivates action.

Now, we can observe versions of this pattern within an ordinary human lifetime.

Craving reinforces habits.

Habits influence perception.

Perception reinforces self-conception.

Self-conception generates more craving.

The radical Buddhist proposition is that this causal process does not necessarily end when the organism dies.

Its karmic continuity can condition another life.

The psychological mechanism is familiar.

The proposed continuation beyond death is not empirically established.

But the philosophical logic becomes clear.

### 5.2 The Dalai Lama explains the mechanism remarkably directly

In his 2011 statement concerning reincarnation and the tulku tradition, the Dalai Lama explicitly distinguishes two kinds of rebirth.

Ordinary beings are reborn involuntarily through karma and disturbing emotions.

Advanced bodhisattvas may take birth voluntarily through compassion and aspiration.

For ordinary rebirth, he describes a sequence in which ignorance generates karmic imprints, and craving and grasping reactivate them.

Those activated potentials condition the next existence. \[[Statement of His Holiness the Fourteenth Dalai Lama, Tenzin Gyatso, on the Issue of His Reincarnation](https://tibetoffice.org/media-press/featured-news/statement-of-his-holiness-the-fourteenth-dalai-lama-tenzin-gyatso-on-the-issue-of-his-reincarnation?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Now that's a striking distinction.

One being is pushed into another existence by causes they haven't mastered.

Another deliberately manifests a new life for the benefit of others.

In one case, rebirth is bondage.

In the other, rebirth is an expression of compassionate freedom.

This is not to suggest that the second mechanism has been scientifically demonstrated.

But it is fundamental to the Tibetan account of the *tulku*\: an accomplished practitioner whose subsequent birth is understood as the continuation of enlightened activity.

And there is a crucial ethical inversion.

The spiritual ideal is not merely getting oneself out of the universe.

It can include returning to help other beings become free.

### 5.3 But who actually gets reborn?

Here comes the nightmare for anybody wanting a neat, permanent identity.

Nobody-if by *nobody* we mean that no independent, immutable self can be identified.

But that does not mean nothing causally continues.

In Tibetan Buddhist philosophy, the conventional person is designated in dependence upon a changing continuum of bodily and mental processes.

The continuity of consciousness is not supposed to be an indivisible soul-substance.

It is a succession of causally related moments.

And the most subtle mental activity plays a particularly important role in certain tantric explanations.

The Dalai Lama has argued that each moment of consciousness requires a preceding, compatible moment of consciousness, leading him to the conclusion that the mental continuum has no discoverable beginning. \[[The Office of His Holiness The Dalai Lama](https://www.dalailama.com/news/2021/a-scientific-investigation-of-meditation-by-Russian-neuroscientists?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

This is a substantive philosophical argument.

But there is also a substantive objection.

Why must a mental event necessarily arise from an earlier mental event?

Why couldn't appropriately organized physical processes produce consciousness?

The Dalai Lama's argument assumes a principle of causal continuity that a physicalist need not accept.

So the argument doesn't, by itself, prove rebirth.

It explains why rebirth becomes compelling within that metaphysical framework.

### 5.4 What happens when someone dies?

In the Tibetan tantric model, ordinary sensory and conceptual functioning progressively disintegrates.

The associated subtle winds undergo transformations.

The most subtle level of consciousness is said to become manifest during the dissolution process.

Unless liberation occurs, karmic conditions support further continuity.

In traditions that recognize a postmortem *bardo*, the mental continuum may undergo experiences interpreted through karmic conditioning before a new birth.

The details differ across Buddhist schools and Tibetan lineages.

The intermediate state is not universally accepted in every Buddhist tradition.

Nor should we treat the famous forty-nine-day descriptions as a demonstrated timetable for what every deceased person experiences.

But the central causal claim is common\:

**Death removes the present gross bodily organization. It does not necessarily remove the ignorance and karmic conditions responsible for further existence.**

That's the terrifying bit.

The body expires.

The causes of bondage haven't.

So the process continues.

### 5.5 Why does rebirth happen in the first place?

Now Buddhism delivers an answer very different from Trika's.

There is no divine decision to create a universe of rebirth.

No original heavenly event explains everything.

No first sinner set the machinery in motion.

In *Saṃyutta Nikāya* 15.1, the Buddha describes saṃsāra as having no discoverable beginning.

Beings wander under the conditions of ignorance and craving.

The discourse does not identify a first lifetime from which the whole process began. \[[Discourse 15.1: Tiṇakaṭṭha-sutta](https://www.wisdomlib.org/buddhism/book/samyuta-nikaya-linked-discourses/d/doc1913551.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

This is important.

The Buddha is principally identifying the conditions that sustain the cycle, not telling a story about a uniquely identifiable historical beginning.

In simplified form\:

Why does suffering continue?

Because its causes continue.

Why is there further birth?

Because the relevant karmic and affective conditions have not ceased.

What is the remedy?

Bring those conditions to cessation.

It's a remarkably practical change of perspective.

Imagine finding yourself trapped in a burning building.

You could spend the next six hours researching the architect, the original construction permit and whether there was a primordial first flame.

Very intellectually stimulating.

But the Buddha would like you to notice that the building is on fire.

The crucial question is not necessarily who constructed the first building.

It's **what keeps the present fire burning-and how it can be extinguished.**

That's the underlying logic of Buddhist liberation.

## VI. The fundamental difference: who started the bloody thing?

Let's finally put the two accounts side by side.

| Question | Trika Śaivism | Tibetan Buddhism |
|--------|--------|--------|
| What is ultimate reality? | Paramaśiva, self-aware universal consciousness | Emptiness of inherent existence; no independently existing universal Self |
| Why is finite existence possible? | Śiva's free self-manifestation | Dependent origination |
| Why does bondage continue? | Self-contraction, ignorance and karma | Ignorance, craving, clinging and karma |
| What continues across lives? | Limited conscious individual associated with subtle embodiment | Causally connected mental continuum, without permanent self |
| What ends involuntary rebirth? | Liberation through realization of one's Śiva-nature | Liberation through the cessation of ignorance and its dependent causes |
| Is death itself liberation? | No | No |

The similarities are tremendous.

But there's a philosophical disagreement hiding underneath them.

Trika provides an ultimate conscious ground of existence.

Buddhism does not require such a ground and, in many of its philosophical formulations, directly challenges the assertion of one.

This creates different explanatory difficulties.

For Trika, we have to ask why perfect divine freedom manifests real suffering.

For Buddhism, we have to ask how mental continuity beyond death can be established without a permanent substrate-and what causal process connects one life to the next.

Neither difficulty is trivial.

And neither can be dismissed through the comforting slogan that all spiritual traditions are ultimately saying exactly the same thing.

They're not.

They may be studying overlapping aspects of experience.

But their metaphysical explanations are genuinely different.

## VII. Why doesn't the modern West believe in rebirth?

Now let's leave the monasteries.

Put down the Sanskrit manuscript.

Walk into a modern neuroscience laboratory.

Ask the researchers what happens to consciousness when the brain is irreversibly destroyed.

Most will not reach for the *Tantrāloka*\.

And there are serious reasons for that.

But first, an important correction.

The West is not uniformly opposed to reincarnation.

Pew Research Center's May 2025 international study found that 31% of American adults believed in reincarnation. European belief rates were generally lower, although the figures varied substantially between countries. \[[Beliefs about the afterlife by region and religion in 35 countries](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/05/06/beliefs-about-the-afterlife/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

So we are talking particularly about the dominant scientific framework and certain cultural traditions, not every Western individual.

### 7.1 Reason one: the brain is extraordinarily important for consciousness

This is the strongest reason for scientific skepticism.

Change the functioning of the brain, and you can radically change conscious experience.

Anaesthesia can profoundly disrupt conscious responsiveness and experience.

Brain injury can alter memory, emotional regulation, personality and cognitive functioning.

Changes in neural network organization are consistently associated with changes in conscious states.

These aren't speculative metaphysical observations.

They are findings supported by extensive experimental and clinical research. \[[Anesthesia and the neurobiology of consciousness](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38579714/?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Personality change after traumatic brain injury: a systematic review and meta-analysis.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42547613/?utm_source=chatgpt.com); [Neural Network Mechanisms Underlying General Anesthesia: Cortical and Subcortical Nuclei](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11625048/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

And the ordinary scientific inference is powerful.

If changing the brain changes experience, and destroying the brain irreversibly destroys the physical organization that supports known human conscious functioning, there is good reason to expect the associated conscious life to cease.

That's a coherent inference.

Not a foolish assumption made by ignorant Westerners.

But now distinguish two claims.

One: ordinary human consciousness depends heavily on the living brain.

This is strongly supported.

Two: no consciousness could possibly exist independently of any biological brain, under any conceivable conditions.

That is a much broader metaphysical claim.

Evidence for the first supports skepticism about disembodied consciousness, but it does not constitute a logical proof of the second.

The gap is important.

However, we mustn't exploit that gap to announce that rebirth is therefore probable.

Not having disproved something is not the same as having established it.

### 7.2 Reason two: no independently verified mechanism of transmission

Suppose a person dies.

They have memories, dispositions, desires and a particular organization of consciousness.

A child is born years later.

What connects the two?

In Trika, the answer involves the conscious individual and subtle body.

In Tibetan Buddhism, it involves a causally connected mental continuum and karmic conditioning.

But how, physically, does this work?

What carries the information?

How is it preserved?

What establishes its relation to one developing organism rather than another?

What allows karmic consequences to persist across bodily death?

No scientifically established mechanism answers these questions.

And notice that saying "consciousness is nonphysical" does not by itself solve them.

A nonphysical explanation still needs to account for how particular continuities and causal relationships operate.

Otherwise we have replaced one mystery with another.

The traditional models offer metaphysical answers, but they have not been validated as physical mechanisms.

### 7.3 Reason three: our cultural history

For centuries, mainstream Christian theological traditions generally emphasized one earthly life followed by judgment, resurrection and an eternal destiny, rather than an indefinite cycle of reincarnations.

Later, modern European scientific culture increasingly explained human experience through biology, psychology and physical causation.

These two histories are quite different.

Christian theology is not materialist neuroscience.

But neither made classical South Asian rebirth theory the standard explanation of personal identity.

So many Western people inherited a framework in which the question is\:

"Does anything survive this one life?"

Rather than\:

"Why am I undergoing another life, and what ends the cycle?"

That's a profound difference in existential starting point.

### 7.4 Reason four: the lack of ordinary memory

Here's the most obvious objection.

If you've lived before, why don't you remember?

Fair question.

The traditions have answers involving changes of embodiment, memory conditions, subtle mental continuity and the obscuring effects of ignorance.

But these explanations introduce additional claims requiring evidence.

They cannot simply be assumed correct.

A more subtle problem also appears.

Suppose the next person possesses none of your memories, none of your current relationships and none of your familiar personality.

Why should we call them *you*?

This is not merely a scientific question.

It's a question about what constitutes personal identity.

And it has no trivial answer even within one ordinary human lifetime.

You do not remember being an infant.

Your body and psychology have changed enormously.

Yet we ordinarily recognize continuity.

The rebirth theories propose extending the conditions of continuity beyond the organism.

But making such an extension philosophically conceivable does not show that it actually occurs.

### 7.5 Reason five: scientific evidence requires more than compelling stories

Which brings us to the part where the discussion gets genuinely interesting.

## VIII. The evidence that keeps the question alive

There is a research tradition examining young children who spontaneously report memories of apparently previous lives.

The most prominent institutional center is the University of Virginia Division of Perceptual Studies\.

Researchers including Ian Stevenson and Jim Tucker have investigated thousands of cases.

In a peer-reviewed article published in February 2025, Tucker summarizes research covering more than 2,500 cases and discusses children whose reported details appeared to correspond with deceased individuals. \[[What do the cases of the reincarnation type tell us about the mind beyond the brain?](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40627512/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

We shouldn't pretend this body of research does not exist.

Nor should we pretend it proves reincarnation.

The question is what the strongest cases establish.

Imagine a young child makes a series of unusual claims.

They identify a distant location.

A previous occupation.

Specific people.

Unusual circumstances surrounding a death.

Investigators later discover that some of these claims correspond to a deceased individual.

That's potentially interesting.

But immediately, scientific questions arise.

Was the child's statement documented before investigators discovered a matching person?

Could information have been transmitted normally?

Did the family know relevant details?

Were incorrect statements recorded as carefully as correct ones?

Was the supposed match selected from many possible candidates?

Could cultural expectations have influenced the child's reports or adults' interpretations?

A systematic review published in *Explore* identified 78 academic studies of claimed past-life memories. Most were observational, and case reports were the most common design. The authors emphasized the need for better methodology. \[[Academic studies on claimed past-life memories: A scoping review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1550830721000951?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

### The especially important evidence problem

Among approximately 2,500 cases in the University of Virginia collection, only a small minority had written records of children's statements made before subsequent verification.

A review by the Society for Psychical Research documents the distinction and explains why such early records are important.

If you record an unusual claim *before* identifying the deceased individual, it's much harder for hindsight and selective memory to contaminate the account. \[[Reincarnation Cases with Records Made Before Verifications](https://psi-encyclopedia.spr.ac.uk/articles/reincarnation-cases-records-made-verifications/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

But even a well-documented match wouldn't automatically settle the question.

It might establish that a child possessed information that is difficult to explain through identified ordinary channels.

To establish rebirth, we'd still need a justified inference that this information came through postmortem continuity of consciousness rather than some other process.

And we would need to determine whether the evidence favors a specific theory of rebirth.

A verified anomalous memory would not, by itself, prove the *puryaṣṭaka*, the Tibetan bardo, the twelve links, or the existence of Śiva.

That's a massive distinction.

### And what about near-death experiences?

In the AWARE-II study, published in *Resuscitation* in 2023, researchers examined consciousness and reported experience during cardiac arrest and resuscitation.

Some survivors reported memories or experiences associated with the period of cardiac arrest.

The study also documented the re-emergence of organized electrical brain activity during CPR in some patients.

Nobody identified the visual target used to test awareness, while one interviewed survivor identified an auditory stimulus. \[[AWAreness during REsuscitation - II: A multi-center study of consciousness and awareness in cardiac arrest](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37423492/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

Fascinating research.

But notice that these were patients undergoing resuscitation, some of whom recovered.

The findings do not establish conscious survival following irreversible death.

And they certainly do not demonstrate rebirth into a new organism.

The evidence therefore leaves us with a nuanced position.

There are phenomena worth investigating.

Some reported cases are difficult to evaluate retrospectively.

Modern neuroscience provides powerful evidence connecting consciousness with brain function.

And there is presently no broadly accepted scientific demonstration of postmortem rebirth.

The intellectually responsible conclusion is not "obviously impossible" or "definitely proven."

It is that the traditional claims remain unestablished, and their strongest potential evidence deserves rigorous assessment.

## IX. Now consider the implications if rebirth actually exists

Forget the scholarly controversy for a moment.

Suppose that the central claim is true.

Some aspect of conscious continuity survives the destruction of the present organism and conditions another life.

Not merely as a metaphor.

Literally.

What follows?

### 9.1 Your death would not guarantee an end to suffering

This is the first-and most disturbing-implication.

Modern people often imagine death as the absolute boundary of personal concerns.

Whatever the difficulties of this life, eventually they're over.

In the rebirth traditions, that consolation is not secure.

Death may end the present episode of suffering.

But it does not necessarily end the conditions producing suffering.

The Buddhist recognizes that another life may arise from unresolved karmic causes.

The Śaiva recognizes that the contracted individual may continue through further embodiment.

The implications are staggering.

You're not merely trying to survive one difficult existence.

You're trying to understand the causes of a potentially vast continuity of existence.

And if ignorance is what sustains that continuity, then understanding consciousness becomes more than an interesting intellectual hobby.

It becomes a question of liberation.

### 9.2 Your present personality might not be the main thing worth preserving

This is where rebirth becomes genuinely unsettling.

Imagine another life really does occur.

But you don't retain your current memories.

You don't retain your name.

You don't retain your appearance.

You might not retain your species.

Within traditional Buddhist cosmology, rebirth is possible in various realms, not only as another human.

Trika's cosmology also includes diverse forms and conditions of embodied existence.

The claim is far more expansive than repeatedly getting another nice human life.

Now ask yourself\:

What exactly are you trying to preserve?

The particular person you've spent decades constructing?

Or the deeper conditions of consciousness that make that person possible?

This doesn't mean your current personality has no value.

It means the traditional path's ultimate objective is not to freeze that personality indefinitely.

For Buddhism, it is liberation from ignorance and suffering.

For Trika, it is recognition of the Self that is not confined to finite personality.

Both displace the ordinary ego from the center of ultimate concern.

### 9.3 The way you treat other beings could acquire cosmic significance

Now suppose there really is a continuity of karmic consequences beyond death.

Then the significance of an action wouldn't necessarily terminate with the biological lifespan of the actor.

This doesn't prove that the universe operates as a perfectly fair moral accounting system.

It doesn't justify telling suffering people that they deserved their circumstances because of a previous life.

That would be morally dangerous and epistemically unwarranted.

But within the traditional framework, intentional action matters beyond its immediate visible consequences.

Cruelty can condition future suffering.

Generosity can condition future well-being.

Habits can reinforce the structures of bondage.

And genuine wisdom can undermine them.

The central concern is not divine punishment.

It is causal conditioning.

The universe isn't necessarily imagined as a celestial policeman.

The problem is that the very qualities you cultivate may participate in the continuity of what you become.

Now connect this with the Buddhist teaching that beings have wandered through beginningless saṃsāra.

In the *Saṃyutta Nikāya*'s discourses on beginningless wandering, the Buddha employs extraordinary images to suggest the vastness of past relationships and suffering.

The point is to develop disenchantment with endless wandering and compassion for the beings caught within it. \[[Samyutta Nikaya: The Grouped Discourses](https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)\]

If such a cosmology were true, every encounter with another living being would become morally charged in a radical way.

The stranger.

The animal.

The person you despise.

The being whose suffering seems remote from your own interests.

Their existence would no longer be philosophically isolated from yours within a single lifetime.

But even without accepting literal rebirth, their suffering would still matter.

The ethical importance of compassion does not depend on demonstrating that someone was once your relative.

### 9.4 Liberation would be the most important achievement imaginable

Now take the hypothesis seriously to its logical conclusion.

Suppose you could become wealthy, famous, brilliant, admired and extraordinarily comfortable.

But none of those accomplishments ended the ignorance responsible for recurring conditioned existence.

Then from the perspective of these traditions, you'd have succeeded magnificently within the system while failing to understand the system itself.

This is not an argument against making money, studying, loving people or participating in the world.

Indeed, Trika affirms the reality of worldly manifestation, and Mahāyāna Buddhism places enormous importance on compassionate action.

The distinction is between participating in life and mistaking its temporary rewards for ultimate liberation.

You could possess everything you ordinarily want and still not know what you are.

You could understand entire philosophical systems and still be governed by craving.

You could master Sanskrit, recite the *Tantrāloka*, study the Six Dharmas and construct the most impressive intellectual account of consciousness imaginable.

And the traditions would still ask\:

**Have you actually understood the ignorance that keeps bondage in place?**

That's the question knowledge alone cannot automatically answer.

## X. But what if rebirth is false?

Now we need the final intellectual correction.

Suppose there is no rebirth.

Suppose consciousness is entirely dependent on biological processes and irreversibly ends with the death of the organism.

Would all this study have been wasted?

Not necessarily.

The analysis of clinging remains relevant.

The distinction between the observing perspective and the objects of experience remains philosophically interesting.

The investigation of habitual identification remains psychologically useful.

The cultivation of compassion, ethical restraint and attentiveness remains valuable.

And the fact of death would make the present life no less important.

Indeed, it might make it even more urgent.

This gives us a powerful practical conclusion under uncertainty.

You do not need to pretend to possess certainty about postmortem existence in order to investigate the causes of suffering.

Nor do you need to believe a particular metaphysical doctrine before examining whether your ordinary sense of identity accurately describes experience.

You can take the question seriously without manufacturing an answer.

You can study the evidence without being credulous.

You can practice without pursuing dangerous experiences or attempting to reproduce extraordinary death rituals.

And you can understand the philosophical stakes without treating every ancient claim as established fact.

The uncertainty itself becomes a reason for intellectual discipline.

## XI. The final word: what is rebirth, really?

After all the texts, the subtle bodies, the Tibetan mental continua, the karmic imprints, the metaphysics of Śiva and the empirical debate, we can finally state the essential disagreement with some clarity.

For Trika, rebirth is the continuation of finite, karmically conditioned experience within the manifestation of universal consciousness.

The deepest Self is not reborn as an independently existing individual substance.

It is Śiva.

What undergoes rebirth is the contracted individual in its conditioned continuity.

And liberation is the recognition that this limited identity never exhausted the true nature of consciousness.

For Tibetan Buddhism, rebirth is the dependent continuation of a mental and karmic process without a permanent owner.

Ignorance and craving condition further becoming.

The process continues insofar as its causes remain operative.

And liberation consists in ending those causes through wisdom.

Neither theory is established by contemporary science.

But both offer radically developed philosophical responses to a question that ordinary assumptions often leave unexplored.

**What would it mean for the process of being someone to continue without the survival of the person exactly as they presently understand themselves?**

That's the central question.

And it's bigger than the image of a soul moving between bodies.

Because it forces us to examine what a self is, what continuity means, and whether consciousness can be adequately explained by the physical organization of one organism.

*Have you recognized the consciousness whose unlimited nature you have mistaken for a finite person?*
